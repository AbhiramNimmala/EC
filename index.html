●

The failure mode is not "too few users." It is zero users plus a polished landing page, which reads as someone who likes building more than selling. That is the most common way a technically strong application dies.

The wedge

Hackathon participants, in the 72 hours after their event ends

Your own pitch names the moment: "I built an app and have no clue what to do next." That moment has a timestamp. A student has just shipped something real, has a Devpost page, has teammates, has momentum — and nowhere to put it. By Monday it dies in a repo.

  • They are enumerable. Devpost publishes every submission from every hackathon with team names and links. A public, structured, continuously-refreshed list of exactly your user at exactly the right moment.

Technical — the next 10 days

Business decisions

  • Do not incorporate. No revenue, no co-founder, no investor requiring it. YC's form handles not-yet-formed companies. Incorporating costs money, creates tax obligations, and proves nothing. Drop "Inc." wherever it still appears.
  • Do not charge yet. Pricing a two-sided platform at 40 users destroys the only thing you have. If someone offers to pay, take it and write it down — a data point, not a model.
  • Decide the one sentence and use it identically everywhere — site, YC form, DMs, LinkedIn. Three different descriptions read as someone who has not decided.
  • Write down every user conversation. Date, name, what they said, what you changed. Worth more than any feature you could ship in the same hours.

  • You will send 30 messages, get 2 replies, and conclude it does not work. A 6% week-one reply rate is normal. It climbs as messages get more specific and the directory looks fuller.

Dated items

A 5–10 page paper written between October and mid-February, and where to put it. The target is AIAA Region I — solo-enterable, no faculty sponsor, drivable, and the manuscript deadline lands inside the application window.

●

Do not write the obvious paper. "I built a 6-DOF simulator, here is its architecture" has no finding, no reviewer can evaluate it, and it reads as a project report with citations stapled on. Descriptive project papers are the most common desk rejection. A paper needs a question that could have come out the other way.

The paper to write

How Much Model Fidelity Does a Sounding-Rocket Trajectory Actually Need?

A Monte Carlo ablation of a 6-DOF simulator.

Collegiate and amateur rocketry teams build increasingly elaborate simulators — full 6-DOF, quaternion attitude, Barrowman aerodynamics, US Standard Atmosphere 1976, active control. Each addition costs implementation time and introduces its own failure modes. Which of them actually change the predicted outcome once realistic dispersions are present?

Contribution, in one sentence: under realistic wind and thrust-misalignment dispersion, [X] dominates predicted landing dispersion, while [Y and Z] — the two most commonly added fidelity features — contribute less than [N]% of total variance. Fill X, Y, Z from what the runs say. Do not decide the answer in advance.

Why this question, for you specifically

  • Answerable entirely with code you already have. No new hardware, no data collection, no faculty lab access. The experiment is a loop.
  • Useful to a named community — student rocketry teams, high-power hobbyists, anyone choosing what to build first. Usefulness to a real audience is what separates a publishable undergraduate paper from an exercise.
  • It can come out either way. If fidelity matters enormously, that is a finding. If most of it washes out, that is a better finding and a mildly contrarian one.
  • It reads as computer science. Numerical integration, error propagation, variance decomposition, software design under uncertainty. Since you transfer as a CS major, frame it as computational science rather than an aerospace side interest.

The experiment

Roughly 25–30 hours across October–January, most of it unattended compute.

Baseline vehicle — fix it in the first week of October

Every week spent deciding which rocket to simulate is a week not spent running. In preference order: a NAR/Tripoli certification-class high-power rocket with a commercial motor (ThrustCurve.org publishes a machine-readable thrust-curve database); your own LiftOff design if the geometry is complete, which links the two projects; or a published Student Launch vehicle with a public technical report. Document mass, length, diameter, fin geometry, motor designation, CG/CP at ignition — that table goes in the paper.

Run one-factor-at-a-time: each low-fidelity option substituted individually, plus both endpoints. About 11 configurations. OFAT alone is defensible and much easier to explain than a factorial design.

★

Use the same seeds across configurations. This is the single most important methodological detail in the paper. Paired sampling means the difference between configurations is measured without sampling noise swamping it, and it lets you report paired statistics instead of comparing two noisy distributions. Say so explicitly in the methods section — reviewers notice.

For wind, pull real vertical profiles from NOAA's Integrated Global Radiosonde Archive — free and public. Real sounding data instead of an invented wind model is a small amount of extra work that substantially raises credibility.

Measure

Apogee altitude (mean and 95% interval) · landing dispersion (95% containment radius, and the ellipse if not circular) · maximum angle of attack and time above a stability threshold · peak lateral load · wall-clock runtime per trial — the cost side of the argument, and nobody reports it.

The analysis that makes it a paper

  1. Variance decomposition. For each axis, how much of total landing-dispersion variance does removing it explain? Sobol analysis is the rigorous version; paired-difference comparison with confidence intervals is the honest simpler one and is adequate at this length.
  2. Cost/benefit. Accuracy gained against runtime and implementation complexity. This is the figure people will remember and cite.
  3. A decision rule. End with something a team can use: "if you build one thing, build X; do not bother with Y until Z." Undergraduate papers rarely close with actionable guidance and it is disproportionately well received.

The numerics section — where the CS shows

  • Integrator convergence. Euler, RK4 and adaptive RK45 across 10 ms down to 0.1 ms. Plot error against step size on log-log and show the observed order. Does RK4 actually achieve 4th order, or does the burnout discontinuity degrade it? It usually does — a nice concrete finding.
  • Quaternion normalization drift. Measure ‖q‖−1 over flight under different renormalization strategies, and the attitude error the drift induces.
  • Event handling. Burnout, apogee and deployment are discontinuities. Fixed step versus root-finding on the event function changes the answer. Most student simulators get this wrong and never find out.
  • Reproducibility. State the seeding strategy and exact versions; publish the code.

Validation

Any real flight data — a Rutgers rocketry club altimeter log, a published Student Launch report, an open dataset — transforms the paper's credibility. Without it, be explicit: this is a relative study between models, not an accuracy claim against reality. Say so plainly rather than hoping nobody asks.

Where to publish — ranked

Tier 1 — no gatekeeping, do these immediately

1. Zenodo — same-day DOI. No moderation, no affiliation requirement, no ORCID. Upload the preprint and the code as separate cross-linked records. A permanent citable DOI in minutes. 2. engrXiv — a real preprint server, ~3 business days. Light moderation only, no endorsement, no institutional gate, run by a 501(c)(3). The correct preprint home for this paper.

A paper whose figures a stranger can regenerate is in the top few percent of undergraduate work — and it is the most persuasive thing you can hand an internship reviewer.

On arXiv — read before you try

!

arXiv is effectively closed to you, and it changed recently. As of an official policy update on January 21, 2026, an institutional email is no longer sufficient on its own. A new submitter needs both a qualifying institutional address and prior authorship on a paper already accepted to arXiv in the relevant domain. Your @rutgers.edu satisfies the first; nothing satisfies the second.

The only remaining route is personal endorsement from an established arXiv author in eess.SY or physics.space-ph. Mechanically: attempt a submission, receive a six-character endorsement code, find a candidate via the "Which of these authors are endorsers?" link on a related paper's abstract page, email them your draft plus the code.

Cold-emailing strangers for this has a poor hit rate and reads badly. The realistic path is Prof. Nagarakatte — as an endorser himself or, more likely, naming a Rutgers colleague in controls or aerospace. Make that ask in October, not January. It takes lead time and it is a favour, not a transaction.

It also may not be worth it. Zenodo + engrXiv + an accepted AIAA conference paper is a stronger, more legible package than an arXiv preprint alone, and it involves no favours.

Skip

  • Journal of Student Research — $299 post-acceptance fee. Pay-to-publish reads poorly.
  • Curieux, The Young Researcher, IJHSR, Journal of Emerging Investigators — high-school-oriented. Publishing as a college student in a high-school journal is a negative signal on a transfer file.
  • jurpress.org — free and open, but roughly nine months to editor assignment. Far outside your window.
  • AIAA SciTech 2027 — closed, abstracts were due May 21, 2026. The route in is winning Region I.
  • IEEE Aerospace 2027 — closed for new submissions, and Big Sky Montana is expensive.

The presentation — build once, reuse everywhere

AIAA Region I is a judged 20-minute oral; NCUR, ASCEND and any meetup want the same material. Twelve slides: title → the scene (students build elaborate simulators) → the question → the vehicle → the simulator → the ablation matrix → dispersion model and the paired-seed trick → dispersion ellipses → variance decomposition → accuracy vs runtime, the money slide → the decision rule → limitations, code link, DOI, contact.

Rehearse to time. Judges score delivery explicitly, and undergraduates lose points almost entirely on running long and on failing to say what they did versus what the tools did.

The 9/11 counterfactual — do not write this as a paper

"What if AI had existed on 9/11" is fundamentally unfalsifiable. No experiment, no dataset, no baseline, no way for a reviewer to evaluate whether you are right. It reads as an essay with citations, and at worst as a student speculating about a national tragedy for application material. Reviewers are unsentimental about this and it can actively hurt you.

There is a rigorous version of the same instinct, and it is worth understanding because it is the motivation rather than the paper: the failure was not a sensing failure. The signals existed. It was an information-fusion and institutional-silo failure — the 9/11 Commission Report is largely a document about data sitting in separate systems that could not be joined. That is a real, studiable problem class. Use it as one framing paragraph of your introduction, never as the paper.

The natural disaster tracker — you already started it

Emberline is this paper. The MODIS/VIIRS hot-spot harmonization you did for NASA Space Apps is a natural-disaster-tracking contribution: real public satellite data, a defined technical problem (cross-sensor calibration between 1 km MODIS and 375 m VIIRS, FRP reconciliation across overpass times), and you have already done the hard part. Do not begin a new tracking project. Write up Emberline — the cheapest real paper available to you, and NCUR (Dec 4) is an ideal home.

The wartime shelter-guidance idea — set it aside for now

Two practical reasons rather than squeamish ones. The data does not exist in a usable form — real-time conflict data is fragmented, access-restricted and often deliberately unreliable; there is no public dataset that would let you validate a "should a civilian shelter now" prediction, and without validation you have an untested system making life-safety claims. And the failure cost is asymmetric in a way an undergraduate project cannot responsibly own; a reviewer who takes it seriously will ask about false negatives and there is no good answer at this scale.

The defensible relative is public alerting and evacuation decision support for natural hazards — wildfire evacuation timing, flood warning lead time — where real data and evaluation protocols exist.

★ Build this one: Clearsky → airspace monitoring

Detecting the Uncooperative: Anomaly Detection in ADS-B Trajectory Data for Airspace Integrity Monitoring

The strongest of the three by a wide margin, and the one to put the semester into if you only do one.

  • The data is free, enormous and public. The OpenSky Network maintains an open research dataset of ADS-B transponder messages — billions of position reports, documented API, historical archives. The rarest thing in undergraduate research: a real, large, credible dataset with no access negotiation.
  • The question is genuinely open. Cooperative aircraft broadcast ADS-B; uncooperative, non-equipped and small UAS do not. So the interesting problem is detecting the presence of something that is not announcing itself, or detecting broadcast behaviour that is anomalous — spoofed positions, implausible kinematics, dropped-then-resumed tracks.
  • It is computer science. Anomaly detection over spatiotemporal trajectory data with a real evaluation protocol. Not aerospace-domain-knowledge gated.
  • It builds on work you already did, so the paper says "I extended my HackGT project into a research result" — a far better narrative than six unrelated projects.
  • The security relevance is real without writing about war. Counter-UAS and airspace integrity are civilian aviation-safety problems first.

Contribution: a reproducible evaluation of trajectory-anomaly detection on open ADS-B data, characterising which classes of airspace anomaly are detectable from cooperative surveillance alone — and which are structurally invisible. That last clause is what makes it honest and interesting: "here is the boundary of what this sensing modality can see" is more defensible than "my detector got 94%."

The plan

Data. OpenSky historical dataset, one busy terminal area, one month. Roughly 107–108 state vectors — large enough to be credible, small enough for one machine.

Anomaly classes to define and detect:

  1. Kinematic implausibility — reported position, velocity and altitude violating a physical flight model. Your 6-DOF work is directly reusable here as the plausibility check, and stating that connection between your two projects is worth doing explicitly.
  2. Track discontinuity — transponder drops and resumes, the signature of both equipment failure and deliberate evasion.
  3. Identity anomalies — ICAO address inconsistencies, callsign/type mismatches.
  4. Behavioural outliers — flight profiles that do not match the distribution for the declared aircraft type or route.

Method. Establish a baseline first — simple physical-plausibility rules and statistical outlier detection — before reaching for anything learned. A paper showing a simple baseline is hard to beat is a good paper; one reporting only a neural network's accuracy with no baseline is not. If you do go learned, an autoencoder or isolation forest over trajectory features is appropriate at this scale.

Evaluation. The hard part, confront it directly: there are no labels. Real anomalies are rare and unannotated. The credible approach is synthetic injection — take real tracks, inject each anomaly class at controlled severity, measure detection rate against false-alarm rate as a function of severity, report ROC curves per class. Then examine the top-ranked real detections by hand and characterise them qualitatively. Say plainly in limitations that synthetic injection is not real adversarial behaviour — reviewers respect stated limitations far more than confidence.

Ethics and scope. One short careful paragraph: this uses only publicly broadcast, unencrypted transponder data intended for collision avoidance, makes no claims about individual aircraft or operators, and is framed as aviation-safety research. Include it — its absence is more conspicuous than its presence.
